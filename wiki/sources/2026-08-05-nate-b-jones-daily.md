# AI Slop Is Costing You Hours. Here's How To Stop Sending It.

## The Big Idea

AI-generated work doesn't eliminate effort — it transfers it invisibly to whoever reads the document next. Nate's core argument: we've solved the blank page but created something worse — finished-looking documents that nobody checked, traveling at machine speed to readers still expected to pay attention and act. The fix is not a better prompt or checklist. It is [[pro-authorship]]: a discipline of owning everything you send before it leaves your hands.

---

## His Argument / Reasoning Chain

1. **The cost-transfer model.** When you send AI-generated work you haven't read, you haven't eliminated the work — you've transferred it to the reader. They now spend the time you saved deciding if the document means anything. At scale, the people who read carefully pay for the people who don't.

2. **The blank-page inversion.** The historical problem was starting. AI solves the blank page. But the new problem is the finished-looking page: it signals completion while leaving the core work (deciding if it says what you mean) undone.

3. **Hill-climbing convergence as the architectural barrier.** LLMs are trained on reward signals that represent "good writing." This means every model climbs the same hill — toward polished, inoffensive, increasingly identical output. Anti-slop checklists identify surface features of a different point on the hill; they don't change the hill or the trajectory.

4. **Why one checklist cannot fix convergence.** A single universal anti-slop checklist applied to all models through all prompts produces a single universal voice — worsening convergence by standardizing the deviation. The checklist describes what the output should look like; authorship is a prior process that shapes what the output should say.

5. **The accountability gap.** AI can make every pass faster. It cannot decide whether the work is worth sending. That decision — and its consequences — remains with the person who hits send.

---

## The Framework: [[pro-authorship]]

Named framework: **Pro-Authorship** (or: "Be Pro-Authorship").

Two interlocking components:

### 1. The Voice Discovery Skill
A structured process for identifying and capturing your authentic voice *before* prompting. Functions as a reference artifact that steers the model toward your perspective rather than the convergence mean. This is the only anti-slop measure that addresses the architectural problem: it doesn't fight the hill, it changes where the model starts climbing from.

### 2. The Accountability Standard
"If you didn't read it, don't send it to me." A named personal principle. Nate frames this as both a professional standard and a career differentiator: in an environment where AI voices are converging, a distinctive, accountable voice becomes a professional signal rivals can't buy from the same API.

## Receipts (Evidence Cited)

- **Deloitte — A$97,587 refunded:** A Deloitte report circulated before anyone checked its numbers. One external academic did — found errors — and Deloitte refunded A$97,587. Leading evidence in the Substack headline.
- **curl maintainer team — hours lost per AI bug report:** Developers paste AI-generated bug reports into the curl open-source project without reading the documentation. Each hallucinated or fabricated report costs the team hours a careful reader would have caught in minutes.
- **Lawyers fined by a judge:** A judge sanctioned lawyers for submitting AI-generated briefs containing fabricated citations. The cost landed on clients — not the lawyers, not the tool — illustrating that accountability follows the sender.
- **Nate's own drafting process:** He cites dozens of drafts before and after AI adoption as evidence that speed and readiness are separate variables. The number of drafts didn't fall; the time per draft did.

## The Contrarian Edge

The consensus take on AI slop: it's a tone-of-voice problem fixable with a better prompt or checklist (avoid "delve," vary sentence length, add personality). Nate's position: that consensus is wrong in its diagnosis. The root cause is the hill-climbing convergence architecture of how LLMs are trained — a structural property, not a stylistic one. No checklist can fix it, because no checklist changes the reward function. The correct intervention is a prior human act (authorship) not a downstream edit (checklist).

---

## What It Means For Leaders

1. **Before your next send, do the read.** "If you didn't read it, don't send it to me" is a named accountability principle — adopt your own and say it out loud.
2. **Build a Voice Discovery process before scaling AI output.** Collect 3–5 authentic samples of your own writing; use them as reference material when prompting — a model of your perspective, not a word-avoid list.
3. **Measure AI productivity in downstream attention cost, not upstream output speed.** "50 emails in an hour" is not a win if each recipient spends 10 minutes finding meaning.
4. **Name your standard explicitly for your team.** The Deloitte and lawyer cases share a failure: no one was explicitly responsible for the read before the send. A stated rule changes who bears that responsibility.

---

## Mistake to Avoid

Treating AI slop as an aesthetic problem (tone, word choice, sentence variety) fixable with a better checklist. The root cause is architectural convergence; the fix is a prior human process (authorship), not downstream editing. Checklists treat symptoms; authorship addresses cause.
