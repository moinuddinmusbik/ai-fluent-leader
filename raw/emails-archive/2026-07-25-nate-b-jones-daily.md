## The Big Idea

When AI drives execution costs toward zero, outputs converge. Every AI-assisted post, product, and pitch starts to rhyme. The reflex is to blame the model and go shopping for a better one — but the sameness follows you there too. Nate's diagnosis: execution was never the differentiator. The moment it became nearly free, value moved upstream to the things no model can hand you — taste, a real point of view, and knowing which thing is even worth making in the first place.

## Why It Matters Now

Fable 5's pricing ($10/M input, $50/M output — double Opus 4.8) turned every leadership meeting into the same meeting: do we have to pay for this, or can we route around it? That routing arithmetic is now finished — the playbook is public, consultants all sell the same one. But once routing discipline is universal (and Nate says that day is close), you're back to competing on cheap execution that everyone else can also run. The homogeneity problem is structural: when anyone can produce competent output at near-zero cost, competent output has near-zero marginal value.

## His Argument

- **Convergence is a fact about tasks, not models:** A $1 model tied a $9 one. Switching to a better model doesn't solve AI sameness — it confirms that execution quality is already commoditized at the task level.
- **Routing advantage will be fully arbitraged:** The routing playbook is public; every consultant sells it. Once every org routes the same way, routing discipline is a floor — not a moat.
- **The real differentiator is "imagination" — two specific, buildable capacities:** Technical imagination (seeing which new work is worth doing when execution goes cheap) and organizational imagination (getting your org to execute tasks the old process would never have generated).
- **Mitchell Hashimoto's $40 experiment proves the point:** Advantage forms at the task-specification layer — asking for something no routing table would ever assign — not at the model-selection layer.
- **The two-layer architecture:** Use the cheapest capable model as your engine (open-weights where possible). Use frontier models as your steering — for creativity, judgment, taste, and novel task specification.

## The Contrarian Edge

The consensus says: pick the right model, build routing discipline, win on cost efficiency. Nate's break: the routing war is already won by everyone simultaneously, which means it's won by no one. Routing discipline in 2026 is a floor, not a ceiling. Almost no one is budgeting for what actually separates outcomes post-routing: organizational capacity to specify novel tasks, and the judgment to know which tasks are worth running. That gap — between orgs that ask for new kinds of work and orgs that run the old list cheaper — is where advantage will concentrate. See also: [[imagination-gap]].

## What It Means For You

1. **Finish routing, then stop talking about it.** Route every task class to the cheapest capable model. This is table stakes. The time spent past diminishing returns belongs on imagination.
2. **Run the $40 question on your team this week.** What would your team do with AI work that cost $40 instead of $4,000? Blank stares = your actual bottleneck is task imagination, not model cost.
3. **Reserve frontier model spend for steering, not engine work.** Use capable models for judgment-heavy, taste-sensitive, novel-task work. Let cheap models handle repeatable execution.
4. **Audit your AI output for sameness.** If your work could swap bylines with competitors, you're competing on execution — the spot that just got commoditized. Build real viewpoint into every AI-assisted process.

## Mistake to Avoid

Chasing a better model to solve an output-sameness problem. The convergence follows you to better models. The problem is upstream of the model: what are you asking for, and does it come from a real point of view? No model hands you taste.
